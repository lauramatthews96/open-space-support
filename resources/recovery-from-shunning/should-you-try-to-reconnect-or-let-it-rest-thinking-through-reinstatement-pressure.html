Should You Try to Reconnect, or Let It Rest? Thinking Through Reinstatement Pressure

7 min read · Last updated August 2026

If you've been shunned, you may feel pressure, from yourself, from family, or from the possibility itself, to consider reinstatement or reconciliation, purely as a way back to contact with the people you love, regardless of whether you actually believe what would be required to return. This is a genuinely difficult decision, and there's no version of this article that tells you the right answer, because it depends entirely on your own circumstances, values, and what you're able to live with. What follows is a way to think it through clearly, rather than a recommendation either way.

Naming the actual dilemma honestly

The core tension usually looks something like this: reinstatement, or expressed willingness to return, may restore contact with people you love, but often requires outward compliance with beliefs or practices you no longer hold, and sometimes requires this regardless of what you privately believe. Weighing "I get my family back" against "I have to misrepresent what I actually believe, indefinitely" is a genuinely hard calculation, and there's no formula that makes it easy.

It's worth being honest with yourself about which version of this you're actually facing. Some people find a way to reconnect with family without fully returning to the group, depending on how strictly shunning is enforced by particular individuals versus formal doctrine. Others find that any contact genuinely does require full, ongoing compliance. Getting clear on which situation you're actually in is a useful first step before weighing the decision itself.

Questions worth sitting with, honestly

What would reinstatement actually require of me, specifically and practically? Not the general idea of it, but the actual, concrete requirements, attendance, statements, behaviour, and how sustainable that would be for you over months and years, not just in the short term relief of regaining contact.

Am I considering this because I've genuinely reconsidered my beliefs, or purely to regain contact? Both are valid starting points, but it's worth being honest with yourself about which one you're actually in, since it changes what the decision would really mean for you long term.

Could I sustain outward compliance without it costing me significantly, emotionally or otherwise? Some people find they can compartmentalise this in a way that feels manageable. Others find the ongoing dishonesty, and the return of surveillance or control, more costly than the contact is worth. Neither answer is wrong, but it's worth trying to predict honestly which one is more likely to be true for you.

What happens to the relationship if I don't pursue reinstatement? Is there any possibility of limited, informal contact without going through formal channels, or is complete separation the only realistic alternative? This affects how much weight the reinstatement question actually carries in your specific situation.

If I imagine myself a year from now, having chosen either path, which one can I more clearly imagine living with? This isn't a definitive test, but it can surface an instinct that's harder to access through purely logical weighing.

There's no universally right answer here, and both choices are valid

Some people do pursue reinstatement, specifically to regain family contact, while privately holding onto their own beliefs, and find a way to make that work for them, at least for a period, or until circumstances change. This is not a failure of integrity. It's a genuinely difficult trade-off that some people decide is worth making, for reasons that make complete sense given how much family and connection matter.

Other people decide that ongoing outward compliance, or repeated scrutiny of their sincerity, would cost them more than the contact is worth, and choose to accept the separation, while grieving it, rather than misrepresent themselves indefinitely. This is equally valid, and equally difficult.

Neither path is a betrayal of yourself. They're both attempts to navigate an genuinely impossible situation, one that was created by the shunning practice itself, not by any failure of yours in how you're responding to it.

If you do consider reinstatement, go in with clear eyes

If you're seriously considering this path, it's worth thinking honestly about what specific version of contact you'd actually get at the end of it, whether that matches what you're hoping for, and whether the relationship, once restored, would need to remain permanently conditional on continued compliance. Some people find the restored relationship genuinely worth the terms. Others find that even restored contact, under those conditions, doesn't provide the closeness or honesty they were actually hoping to regain, and the disappointment of that can be its own significant loss.

If you decide not to, that's not giving up on your family

Choosing not to pursue reinstatement, or deciding you're not willing to misrepresent your beliefs to regain contact, doesn't mean you've stopped loving or valuing the relationships you've lost. It means you've made a genuinely difficult choice about what you're able to live with, and that choice deserves respect, including from yourself, rather than guilt for not having "tried harder" to get your family back on terms that would have required real cost to your own integrity.

This decision can also change over time

You don't have to decide this once and hold that decision forever. Some people reconsider this question at different points in their life, as circumstances change, as parents age, as new information emerges, or simply as their own feelings shift. Whatever you decide now doesn't have to be the final word on it, and giving yourself permission to revisit the question later, without judging your earlier decision, is a reasonable way to hold this ongoing, difficult situation.

Neither path is a betrayal of yourself. They're both attempts to navigate a genuinely impossible situation, one that was created by the shunning practice itself.

If this feels too hard to think through alone

This is a genuinely difficult, sensitive decision, and if you're finding it hard to think through alone, talking to a therapist who understands religious trauma and family estrangement specifically, or to others who've navigated exactly this question, can help more than working through it in isolation.
