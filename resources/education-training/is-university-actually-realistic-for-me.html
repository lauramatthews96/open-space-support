Is University Actually Realistic for Me?

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

If nobody in your life has been to university, and if higher education was never presented as a real option for you growing up, the whole idea can feel like it belongs to a different kind of person, not something genuinely available to you. It's worth separating the parts of that feeling that reflect real, practical barriers from the parts that are just unfamiliarity, because the two often get tangled together, and they need different answers.

The real barriers, and how solvable they actually are

Not having traditional qualifications. This is the one that stops most people before they even look further, but it's also one of the most solvable. Access to Higher Education Diplomas exist specifically for adults without A-Levels, are widely accepted by universities, and our guide on going back to school as an adult covers this route in detail. This barrier is real, but it has a well-established solution.

Money. This is a genuine concern, but it's worth knowing the actual mechanics before assuming it's impossible. Tuition fees for most UK undergraduate degrees are covered by student finance loans, which you don't pay upfront and only start repaying once you're earning above a set income threshold, currently well above minimum wage. Maintenance loans (and in some cases grants, depending on your circumstances) are also available to help with living costs. University is rarely genuinely free of financial pressure, but "I can't afford it" is often less true, in the upfront sense, than it initially appears.

Time and existing responsibilities. If you're already working, or supporting yourself financially, a full-time degree might not be realistic right now, and that's a legitimate practical constraint, not a failure. Part-time degrees, and the Open University specifically, offer flexible routes that let you study around existing work and life commitments, often over a longer period, which may fit your actual circumstances better than a traditional three-year full-time course.

Not knowing how any of it works. Applications, personal statements, UCAS, student finance forms, none of this is intuitive if nobody around you has done it before. This is a genuinely solvable barrier through research and support, not a sign the whole path isn't meant for you. University and college outreach teams, and organisations like the National Careers Service, exist specifically to help first-generation applicants navigate this unfamiliar process.

The feelings that aren't really about barriers

"People like me don't go to university." This is an identity belief, not a factual barrier, and it's worth examining directly rather than treating it as settled fact. University populations include a wide, genuinely diverse range of backgrounds, including a significant and growing number of mature students and first-generation students specifically. You wouldn't be the anomaly you might be imagining.

"I'll be too old, surrounded by eighteen year olds." On many courses, and especially part-time, evening, or Open University routes, this simply isn't accurate, mature students are common, and many courses have a genuinely mixed age range. Even on a traditional campus course, being an older student is generally treated as unremarkable by staff and other students alike, not something you'll be singled out for.

"I won't be smart enough." Academic ability isn't fixed, and it isn't something you either have or don't have permanently. A lot of academic confidence is built through practice and exposure, things you may simply not have had the chance to build yet, rather than a ceiling on what you're capable of.

A realistic way to think it through

Rather than asking "is university realistic for me" as one big, intimidating question, it can help to break it into smaller, more answerable pieces. What subject would I actually want to study, if qualifications weren't a factor yet. What route would get me the entry qualifications I need, and roughly how long would that take. What would the financial reality actually look like, once I understand student finance properly rather than guessing. And what pace, full-time, part-time, Open University, would actually fit my current life.

Answering these one at a time tends to turn an overwhelming question into a series of much more manageable, researchable ones.

It's also completely fine if the answer is "not right now," or "not at all"

None of this is meant to suggest university is the right or necessary path for everyone. Plenty of genuinely fulfilling, successful careers don't involve a degree, and vocational or apprenticeship routes (covered in our guide on vocational training and apprenticeships as an adult) may suit you far better, depending on what you're actually aiming for. The point of this article isn't to push you toward university, it's to make sure that if you're ruling it out, you're ruling it out because it's genuinely not the right fit, rather than because it never felt like it was allowed to be an option in the first place.
