Filling Educational Gaps Without Going Back to a Classroom

5 min read · Last updated August 2026

Not everyone is ready, or wants, to commit to a formal course right away. If you're not sure where to start, or you'd rather build some confidence and knowledge privately before stepping into a classroom setting, there's a genuinely large amount you can do entirely on your own terms, at your own pace, for free or very low cost.

Why this route is worth taking seriously, not just as a stopgap

Self-directed learning isn't a lesser version of formal education, it's a completely legitimate way to build real knowledge and skills, and for a lot of people it's actually a better starting point, because it removes the pressure of grades, deadlines, or being watched by other students while you're still finding your footing. Some people use this route as a bridge toward eventual formal study. Others simply use it to build the general knowledge and confidence they feel they missed out on, without ever needing it to lead anywhere more formal.

Free platforms worth knowing about

Khan Academy covers a huge range of subjects, particularly strong for maths, science, and core academic fundamentals, structured in a clear, step-by-step way that works well if you're starting from a genuinely basic level and want to build up gradually.

The Open University's free courses (OpenLearn) offer short, genuinely university-quality courses across a wide range of subjects, completely free, with no commitment beyond your own interest. This is also a good way to get a feel for how OU study works, useful information if formal study is something you're considering for the future.

Future Learn partners with UK universities to offer free short courses on specific topics, a good option if there's something particular you want to understand rather than a broad subject area.

Your local library is a genuinely underused resource here. Most UK libraries offer free access to online learning platforms (LinkedIn Learning is commonly available through library membership), as well as physical books, quiet study space, and often free basic computer skills sessions for anyone who needs them.

YouTube, used deliberately rather than passively, has genuinely excellent educational content on almost any subject, from academic topics to practical skills, often taught by working professionals or specialist educators, completely free.

A practical way to approach this

Pick one specific gap, rather than trying to cover everything at once. "I want to understand basic maths properly" is more achievable than "I want to catch up on everything I missed," and finishing something specific builds real momentum you can carry into the next thing.

Set a small, consistent amount of time, rather than waiting for a big block of free time. Twenty minutes a few times a week, done consistently, builds more real progress than waiting for a free afternoon that may not come often.

Keep some kind of simple record of what you've covered. A notebook, a document, even a basic list of course titles completed. This isn't for anyone else, it's so you can see your own progress clearly, which matters more for motivation than people expect, especially when you're doing this without the external structure of grades or deadlines.

Don't worry about doing it in the "correct" order. Follow your actual curiosity where it leads, even if it means jumping between subjects rather than working through one in a neat, structured sequence. Sustained interest tends to produce more learning than a technically correct order that you lose motivation for halfway through.

When you might want to move toward something more formal

Self-directed learning has real limits, it generally doesn't come with a recognised qualification, and it can be harder to stay consistent without any external structure or accountability. If you find yourself wanting proof of what you've learned, whether for a job application or your own sense of achievement, or if you're finding it hard to stay motivated without any structure, that's often a natural signal that a more formal course, even a short, low-commitment one, might be a useful next step. Our guides on going back to school as an adult and vocational training and apprenticeships as an adult cover those more structured routes when you're ready for them.

This counts, even without a certificate at the end

It's worth saying plainly: teaching yourself something, consistently and on your own initiative, is a genuinely real form of learning, even without a formal qualification attached to it. The knowledge and confidence you build this way is real, and it often makes the eventual step into formal education, if you take it, considerably less intimidating, because you'll already have some practice at learning something new as an adult, on your own terms.
