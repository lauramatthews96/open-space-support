Talking to Your Children About Why Things Have Changed

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

If you're leaving, or have left, and you have children, one of the hardest parts is figuring out how to explain something huge to them without frightening them, without putting them in the middle of adult conflict, and without expecting them to understand things that took you years to work through yourself. There's no single right script for this conversation, but there are some genuinely useful principles that can help you find your own version of it.

Start with what they actually need to know, not everything you know

Children don't need the full weight of your reasoning, your doubts, or the specifics of what you've come to believe or not believe. What they generally need is much simpler: reassurance that they're safe, that they're loved, and that the big changes happening around them aren't their fault and aren't something they caused.

A version of this that works for a lot of families, adjusted to your child's age: "Some things in our family are changing, and that might feel confusing. It's not because of anything you did. I love you, and that's not changing, no matter what else does."

You can always add more detail as they get older and ask more specific questions. You don't have to explain everything at once.

Match the explanation to their age, roughly

Young children mostly need consistency and reassurance rather than explanation. Simple, concrete statements work better than abstract ones. "We're not going to that building anymore" lands better than a conversation about theology they're not equipped to process.

Older children and pre-teens can usually handle a bit more, particularly around why family relationships might be changing, but still don't need the full adult version. "I've decided some of the things I was taught aren't things I believe anymore, and that means some things about our life are different now" is honest without being overwhelming.

Teenagers often want, and can handle, more of the real picture, especially if they're old enough to have questions of their own. Being honest with a teenager, including admitting uncertainty or that you're still figuring some of it out yourself, tends to build more trust than presenting a falsely confident, fully resolved version of events.

Whatever their age, follow their lead on how much they want to know. Some children ask a lot of questions immediately. Others need time before they're ready to ask anything at all, and that's fine too.

Don't put them in the middle

If there's another parent, grandparent, or family member still in the group, it can be tempting, understandably, to want your child to see your side of things clearly. But children caught in the middle of adult conflict, even conflict that feels justified, tend to carry real stress from it, regardless of who's "right."

Where possible, try to keep the explanation focused on your own choices and feelings, rather than criticising the other adults involved, even if you privately feel strongly about it. "Grandma still believes what she's always believed, and that's her choice, just like this is mine" keeps a child out of a loyalty conflict they didn't ask for and shouldn't have to manage.

It's okay to say you don't have all the answers

Children, especially older ones, often respect honesty about uncertainty more than a falsely tidy explanation. "I'm still figuring some of this out myself, and that's okay, adults don't always have everything worked out" is a genuinely useful thing to model, and it takes pressure off you to have a perfect answer ready for every question.

Watch for how they're processing it, not just what they say

Children don't always express confusion or distress directly. It might show up as behaviour changes, sleep trouble, clinginess, or acting out, rather than through words. If you notice this, it's worth gently checking in, "I've noticed you've seemed a bit different lately, is there anything on your mind about all the changes," rather than assuming silence means everything is fine.

If a child seems to be struggling significantly, a child psychologist or family therapist experienced with religious transition specifically can help, more than trying to manage it entirely alone.

Grief is allowed here too, for them and for you

Your children may be losing relationships, routines, and community too, grandparents, friends, a familiar rhythm to their week. Their grief is real, even if it looks different from yours, and it's worth acknowledging directly rather than assuming they're too young to feel loss. "I know you miss X, that makes complete sense, I miss things too" validates what they're going through without requiring you to have a solution for it.

You're allowed to get this imperfect

There's no perfect way to have this conversation, and you'll likely have some version of it more than once, as your children grow and ask new questions at different ages. Being present, honest within reason, and consistently reassuring matters far more than getting the exact words right in any single conversation.

Children are generally more resilient than we give them credit for, especially when they feel safe and genuinely loved through the process, which you're clearly trying to give them.
