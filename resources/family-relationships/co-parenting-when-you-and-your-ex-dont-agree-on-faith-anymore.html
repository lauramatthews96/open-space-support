Co-Parenting When You and Your Ex Don't Agree on Faith Anymore

7 min read · Last updated August 2026

Separating from a partner is hard enough on its own. When that separation also involves one of you leaving a high-control group and the other staying, co-parenting afterward comes with a layer of complexity most parenting advice doesn't account for. You're not just negotiating school pickups and holiday schedules, you're navigating genuinely different value systems, different ideas about what's appropriate for your children, and sometimes a former partner who sees your departure as a threat to your children's wellbeing rather than a personal decision.

This is difficult territory, and there's no single approach that resolves all of it. But there are some things worth thinking through carefully.

Try to separate your feelings about the group from decisions about your children

This is hard, and it's worth naming that directly. You may have very strong, well-founded feelings about the environment your children are being raised in part-time. But co-parenting generally works better, and holds up better legally if it comes to that, when decisions are framed around your child's specific wellbeing rather than around your view of the group in general terms.

"I'm concerned that missing school events for meetings is affecting her friendships" is a specific, child-focused concern. "I don't want her raised in that environment at all" is a values statement that's much harder to act on practically, and can escalate conflict without necessarily achieving anything for your child directly.

Know what you can and can't control

You generally can't control what happens during your ex's parenting time, within the bounds of the law, even if you strongly disagree with it. Attending religious meetings, being taught particular beliefs, dressing or behaving in certain ways, these are usually within a parent's legal right to decide during their own time with a child, even if you find it difficult to accept.

What you often can influence, sometimes through direct conversation and sometimes through formal custody arrangements, includes things like: medical decisions, school attendance and educational choices, exposure to anything genuinely harmful or unsafe (as opposed to simply different from your own values), and the overall structure of contact time.

Knowing this distinction early can save a lot of energy spent fighting battles that a family court is unlikely to intervene in, and focusing that energy instead on the things that are actually within reach.

Keep communication as businesslike as it needs to be

If communication with your ex is difficult, charged, or occasionally hostile, it's often easier for both of you, and for your children, if you keep necessary co-parenting communication factual and brief. Some separated parents find a co-parenting app (several exist specifically for this, keeping messages logged, scheduling clear, and reducing back-and-forth conflict) genuinely useful for keeping things calm and documented.

You don't need your ex to understand or agree with your decision to leave in order to co-parent functionally. You need a working, if imperfect, system for managing your children's practical needs together.

Prepare your children for two different worlds, without asking them to judge between them

Children in this situation are often moving between two environments with real differences, in belief, in rules, sometimes in what's discussed openly. It generally helps them more to hear something like "things are a bit different at Mum's and a bit different at Dad's, and that's okay, you don't have to choose a side" than to hear either parent criticising the other's household directly.

This is genuinely hard when you feel strongly that one environment isn't good for your child. But children who feel forced to choose a side, or to report back on one household to the other, tend to carry a real emotional burden from that position, regardless of how justified either parent's concerns might be.

If things become genuinely difficult, get proper support

Family mediation can help when communication has broken down but both parents are willing to work toward a functional arrangement. It's not right for every situation, particularly if there's any element of coercive control or abuse involved, but for many separated parents navigating a values divide, it provides a structured, neutral space to work through disagreements.

Family law solicitors experienced with religious or belief-based custody disputes are worth seeking out specifically, rather than a general family solicitor, if things escalate to formal proceedings. This is a genuinely specialised area, and not every solicitor will have handled a case quite like yours.

Therapy, for you and potentially for your children, particularly with someone who understands religious trauma and its impact on family systems, can help everyone involved process what's often a genuinely painful, ongoing situation, not a single event with a clean resolution.

Your children can hold two truths at once

Even in a difficult co-parenting situation, most children are capable of loving both parents while understanding, in an age-appropriate way, that their parents see some things differently now.

Your job isn't to win them over to your view or protect them from ever encountering the other perspective, it's to make sure they feel safe, loved, and not responsible for managing the adult conflict around them. That's a genuinely difficult standard to hold to consistently, especially when you're hurting too, but it's the thing most likely to serve your children well over the years ahead, regardless of how the bigger disagreement between you and your ex ever resolves.
