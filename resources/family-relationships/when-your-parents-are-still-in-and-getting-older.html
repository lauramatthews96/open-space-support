When Your Parents Are Still In and Getting Older

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

There's a specific, quiet grief that comes with watching your parents age while they're still in the group you left, especially if your relationship with them is limited, strained, or shaped by shunning. You may be thinking about their health, their eventual death, and the reality that you might not get the closeness with them in these years that you'd want, or that they might not be there for you in the way a parent normally would be, right when that starts to matter more, not less.

This is a genuinely hard, often under-acknowledged form of loss. It doesn't fit neatly into either "grief" or "estrangement" as most people understand those words, it's something that sits uncomfortably between the two, ongoing and unresolved rather than a single event you can process and move past.

The specific shape of this grief

Unlike a bereavement, where the loss is total and, in a strange way, final, this kind of loss is often partial and ongoing. Your parents might still be alive, might even still be in occasional contact, while the relationship you'd want with them, close, unconditional, uncomplicated, may not be available to you. That ambiguity, the sense of a relationship that's neither fully present nor fully gone, is its own particular kind of difficult.

It's also common to feel a kind of anticipatory grief, grieving in advance for a version of loss that hasn't happened yet, worrying about how their eventual death will unfold given the current state of the relationship, whether you'll be told, whether you'll be welcome at a funeral, whether reconciliation might still happen before it's too late. Thinking about all of this while your parents are still alive is exhausting, and it's a real and valid thing to be carrying, even though it doesn't look like grief in the way people usually picture it.

You're allowed to want a relationship with them, even after everything

It's common to feel some guilt or confusion over still wanting closeness with parents who've participated in shunning you, or who continue to prioritise the group's rules over the relationship. Wanting connection with a parent, even an imperfect or currently unavailable one, doesn't undo anything about your decision to leave, and it doesn't mean you're being pulled back toward beliefs you've moved away from. It just means they're your parents, and that bond doesn't switch off cleanly just because the circumstances around it are painful.

A few ways people navigate this, though none of them fully resolve it

Keeping a door open, without waiting by it. Some people choose to maintain occasional, low-pressure contact, a card at a birthday, a brief message now and then, without expecting it to become a full relationship, and without organising their emotional life around whether or when it might change. This isn't giving up. It's finding a way to hold the possibility open without it consuming you.

Getting practical information sorted early, if you can. If contact is limited, it's worth thinking practically, however difficult, about things like how you'd find out about a serious health decline or a death, whether there are other family members or friends who'd let you know, and whether you want to leave any instructions with them about being informed. This isn't morbid, it's a reasonable response to a genuinely uncertain situation, and having it sorted in advance can reduce panic later.

Processing the anticipatory grief now, rather than only when something happens. Talking to a therapist, ideally one who understands religious trauma and estrangement specifically, about the grief you're already carrying, rather than waiting for a crisis to process it, can make an eventual loss somewhat more bearable when it does come.

Finding support from people who understand this specific situation. This is a strange, isolating kind of loss to explain to someone who hasn't lived something similar. Other former members going through the same thing, whether through a support group or an organisation like the ones in our external resources, can offer a kind of understanding that's hard to find elsewhere.

If reconciliation happens, it might not look how you imagined

Sometimes, later in life, a parent does soften, particularly around serious illness or the birth of grandchildren, moments that can shift priorities even within a rigid framework. If that happens, it's worth knowing it may come with continued limits or conditions rather than a full, unconditional return to closeness. That can be genuinely painful in its own way, a partial repair rather than the complete one you might have hoped for. It's okay to feel both grateful for whatever connection becomes possible and still grieve what remains out of reach.

This is one of the harder, less talked about parts of leaving

Most conversations about leaving a high-control group focus on the early period, the practical rebuilding, the immediate emotional impact. Fewer talk about what it's like years later, watching parents age with the relationship still unresolved.

If this is where you are, it's worth knowing you're not alone in it, even though it can feel like a strange, hard to name kind of loss. It's real, it deserves care and support, and there's no timeline by which you're supposed to have made peace with it.
