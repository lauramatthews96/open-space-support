Unlearning Shame That Was Taught as Fact

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

There's a difference between shame that arises from something you've done, and shame that was installed deliberately, over years, about something you simply are. If you grew up in an environment that taught, explicitly and repeatedly, that your sexuality or gender identity was wrong, sinful, or something to be corrected, the shame you may still be carrying isn't really a reaction to anything, it's the residue of years of direct instruction. That distinction matters, and it's worth understanding clearly, because it changes what actually needs to happen to move through it.

Why this kind of shame is different from ordinary self-doubt

Most shame responds, eventually, to evidence. You feel bad about something, you gather new information or perspective, and the feeling gradually shifts. Shame that was taught as religious or moral fact behaves differently, because it wasn't built from your own experience or reasoning in the first place. It was installed, deliberately, often before you had the tools to evaluate or question it, and reinforced consistently by people and structures that held real authority in your life.

This means unlearning it isn't simply a matter of finding better arguments, though understanding is part of it. It's closer to retraining a deep, automatic response that was built the same way any belief is built through years of repetition, regardless of whether it was ever true.

Recognising the shame for what it is, and where it came from

A useful first step is simply naming this clearly, even privately. This feeling isn't a fact about me. It's something I was taught, deliberately, by people and a system that had specific reasons for teaching it. That doesn't mean the feeling disappears the moment you say it. But it starts to separate the shame from your actual identity, treating it as something external that was placed on you, rather than something inherent that needs fixing.

It can also help to get specific about the actual teaching, rather than leaving the shame as a vague, undefined weight. What, precisely, were you taught, and by whom, and using what reasoning. Looking at it directly, rather than letting it stay as an unexamined feeling, often reveals how much of it rests on interpretation and doctrine rather than on anything true about you as a person, worth examining with the same scrutiny you'd apply to any other belief you were handed rather than chose.

The body holds this even after the mind lets go

It's common to intellectually reject the teaching, to genuinely believe, consciously, that there's nothing wrong with who you are, while still feeling a physical flinch of shame in certain moments, a public display of affection, a conversation about your identity, an unexpected reminder of the old teaching. This gap between intellectual understanding and felt experience is normal, not a sign that the intellectual work hasn't worked. Bodies tend to hold conditioning longer than minds do, and this kind of shame was often reinforced with real emotional and social weight over a long period, which takes genuine time and repetition to unwind, not just correct information.

What tends to actually help

Exposure to the ordinary, unremarkable version of your identity. A lot of shame is sustained by the sense that your identity is uniquely fraught, secret, or exceptional. Spending time in genuinely ordinary LGBTQ+ spaces, where being who you are is simply unremarkable, can do more to shift ingrained shame than any amount of private reasoning, because it offers direct, lived evidence against what you were taught, rather than just an argument.

Talking to others who've navigated the same specific combination. Shame that was taught through a religious framework specifically responds well to connection with others who understand that exact origin, rather than general LGBTQ+ support alone. There's something particular about the doctrinal shape of this shame that's often best understood by people who've carried the same specific kind.

Therapy with someone who understands both religious trauma and LGBTQ+ identity. This combination is specific enough that general therapy, however good, may not fully address it. A therapist with genuine experience in both areas, some are listed in our external resources, can help address the shame at the root, rather than only managing its symptoms.

Practising self-compassion deliberately, even when it feels unfamiliar. If kindness toward yourself, specifically around this part of your identity, feels foreign or undeserved, that discomfort is itself worth noticing rather than avoiding. Small, repeated moments of choosing compassion over the old automatic shame response, gradually build a new pattern, even when it feels unnatural at first.

This shame was never actually about you

It's worth returning to this, directly, as often as you need to: the shame you may still carry was built by a system, using specific teachings, for its own reasons, not discovered as some accurate reflection of who you actually are. You didn't do anything to deserve carrying this. You were taught it, deliberately, the way anyone can be taught almost anything given enough repetition and authority behind it. Unlearning it is real, difficult work, but it is genuinely possible, and a great many people have done exactly this before you, and gone on to feel, eventually, simply and quietly at peace with who they are.

If this feels like more than you can work through alone

If this feels like more than you're able to work through alone right now, please reach out.
