Leaving When You're Also Coming Out

7 min read · Last updated August 2026

For a lot of people, leaving a high-control religious group is already one of the biggest upheavals a life can hold. If you're also coming out, either around the same time or as part of the same process, you're not going through one hard thing, you're going through two, often at once, and often without the support that either one would normally bring on its own.

This combination deserves its own space, because it doesn't behave like either experience alone. It has its own particular shape, and its own particular kind of isolation.

Why this often means losing support from two directions

Most people going through a difficult life change can lean on some part of their existing world while they rebuild another. Someone leaving a religious group might lean on family for emotional support while they figure out the practical side. Someone coming out might lean on their existing friendships and community while they navigate their family's reaction.

If you're doing both at once, and both are connected to the same environment, that safety net often isn't there in either direction. The people who might have supported your exit from the group may be the same people rejecting your identity. The people who might have celebrated you coming out may not exist yet, because your social world was largely contained within the group you're also leaving. You can end up navigating both processes with almost no one who fully understands either one, let alone both together.

The doctrinal layer makes this different from a general family rift

If your religious environment specifically taught that being LGBTQ+ was wrong, sinful, or something to be corrected, the rejection you might face isn't just personal disapproval, it's often framed by your family as a moral or spiritual obligation. That distinction matters. It doesn't make the rejection hurt less, but it does explain why it can feel so total, so resistant to the kind of gradual softening that sometimes happens with other family disagreements. When rejection is taught as righteousness, it can be harder for family members to see a path back to acceptance, even ones who love you and are genuinely struggling with the conflict internally.

It's common to feel like you have to prioritise one identity over the other

Some people in this situation feel pressure, from themselves or from others, to treat one part of this as the "real" issue and the other as secondary. Am I really leaving because of my sexuality or gender identity, or because of everything else. Is this really about faith, or is it really about who I am. You don't have to resolve this into one clean narrative. Both things can be entirely true and entirely intertwined, and there's no requirement to rank them or separate them cleanly for anyone else's benefit, including your own.

Grief and relief can be especially tangled here

Leaving often brings a mix of grief and relief, and coming out often brings a similar mix, fear alongside a sense of finally being able to breathe. When both are happening together, these feelings can compound in ways that are genuinely hard to untangle. You might feel a huge wave of relief at finally being able to live honestly, alongside a grief so large it's hard to locate its edges, for family, community, certainty, an entire structure of belonging, all at once. Both are real. Neither cancels the other out.

Finding the right kind of support

Look for support that understands both experiences, not just one. A general LGBTQ+ support service may not fully grasp the specific dynamics of leaving a high-control religious group, and a general ex-religious support service may not have deep understanding of LGBTQ+ specific needs. Where possible, look for organisations with experience in both, our external resources below include options with that specific overlap.

Give yourself permission to go at your own pace on each front. You don't have to be fully "out" everywhere at once, and you don't have to have fully processed leaving before addressing your identity, or the reverse. These processes can unfold at different speeds, in different spaces, and that's a reasonable way to manage something this large.

Build new community deliberately, rather than waiting for it to appear. If your existing social world was tied to the group, finding LGBTQ+ community and spaces, even slowly, becomes a genuinely important part of rebuilding, not a separate task from leaving, but part of the same overall process. Our guide on finding LGBTQ+ community when you missed out on it growing up goes into this further.

You don't have to justify either part of this to anyone

You're allowed to leave a religious environment for your own reasons, whatever they are. You're allowed to be who you are, without it needing to be balanced against, or justified alongside, anything else. Both of these are simply true, on their own terms, and neither one needs the other's permission to be valid.

This is a lot to carry, and it's carryable

If you're in the middle of both of these processes right now, it's worth knowing plainly that this is genuinely one of the harder combinations someone can go through, not because you're not resilient enough, but because it truly is a lot, happening at once, often without an obvious support structure built for it. That doesn't mean it isn't survivable, or that a genuinely good life isn't on the other side of it. It means it deserves real patience, real support, and time, without pressure to have it all resolved on any particular schedule.

Both of these are simply true, on their own terms, and neither one needs the other's permission to be valid.

If this combination of change feels particularly hard right now

This is a sensitive topic, and if you're finding this combination of change particularly hard to carry right now, please don't do it alone. Switchboard LGBT+ (0800 0119 100, free, confidential) offers support specifically for LGBTQ+ people, and our external resources below include further specialist options.
