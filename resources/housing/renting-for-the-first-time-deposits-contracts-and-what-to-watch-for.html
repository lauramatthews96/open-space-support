Renting for the First Time: Deposits, Contracts, and What to Watch For

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

If you're renting your own place for the first time, the whole process can feel like it's designed to be confusing on purpose. Deposits, contracts, references, fees that appear from nowhere, it's a lot to take in when nobody's walked you through it before. This is genuinely learnable, and most of it comes down to a handful of things worth understanding clearly before you sign anything.

Before you view anywhere, know your budget properly

Work out what you can actually afford, not just in rent, but in total. A common rough guide is keeping rent to around a third of your take home income, though this varies a lot depending on where you live and what else you're managing financially. Remember to budget for bills separately, unless a listing specifically says they're included, gas, electricity, water, internet, and council tax are usually on top of the rent itself, not part of it.

Understanding the deposit

Most landlords in England and Wales ask for a deposit, usually equivalent to five weeks' rent, before you move in. By law, this deposit must be protected in a government-approved scheme (the three main ones are the Deposit Protection Service, MyDeposits, and the Tenancy Deposit Scheme) within 30 days of you paying it. Your landlord or letting agent must give you details of which scheme it's protected in.

This matters because it's what protects your deposit, and your right to get it back at the end of the tenancy, minus any genuinely fair deductions for damage beyond normal wear and tear. If a landlord asks for a deposit but doesn't protect it properly, that's actually against the law, and Shelter (see our external resources) can advise you on what to do if this happens.

Reading the tenancy agreement properly

It's tempting to skim a tenancy agreement and just sign it, especially if you're excited about a place or worried about losing it to someone else. It's worth taking the time to actually read it, though, since it's a legally binding document covering things like:

How long the tenancy runs for, usually starting with a fixed term (commonly six or twelve months), after which it often becomes a rolling monthly arrangement unless otherwise stated.

Who's responsible for what, repairs, maintenance, and who to contact when something goes wrong. Your landlord is legally responsible for keeping the property safe and in good repair, regardless of what the contract says, this is a baseline right you have regardless.

Any restrictions, on pets, guests staying over, or changes you can make to the property. These vary a lot between landlords, and it's worth knowing them upfront rather than discovering a restriction after you've moved in.

Notice periods, how much notice you need to give if you want to leave, and how much your landlord needs to give you.

If anything in the agreement is unclear, it's completely reasonable to ask the landlord or agent to explain it, or to get free advice from Shelter before signing, rather than just hoping it's fine.

Red flags worth watching for

Being asked to pay before you've even viewed the property. This is a common scam pattern, never send money for a deposit or holding fee for a property you haven't actually seen in person.

Landlords or agents who won't provide their details, or seem reluctant to put things in writing. Legitimate landlords and agents are generally happy to confirm things properly, in writing, since it protects them too.

Being pressured to decide or pay immediately, with no time to think it over. Genuine urgency in a competitive rental market does happen, but real pressure to skip reading the contract or verifying details is worth treating with real caution.

Fees that aren't allowed. Under the Tenant Fees Act, most upfront fees beyond the deposit and first month's rent are actually banned in England. If you're asked for admin fees, referencing fees, or similar charges, it's worth checking whether they're legally allowed before paying.

Your rights as a tenant

Regardless of what's in your specific contract, you have certain baseline legal rights, including the right to live in a property that's safe and in good repair, the right to see your landlord's gas safety certificate, and protection from unfair eviction. Shelter's website has clear, free, up to date information on exactly what these rights involve, worth bookmarking before you need it, not just when a problem arises.

This gets easier after the first time

The first time you rent somewhere, everything feels unfamiliar and slightly overwhelming, references, guarantors if you need one, understanding what's normal versus what's a red flag. By the second or third time, most of this becomes second nature. Take your time with this first one, ask questions freely, and don't feel any pressure to pretend you already know things you genuinely haven't had the chance to learn yet.

If your housing situation is urgent

If you're facing homelessness, have nowhere safe to stay tonight, or think something about your tenancy isn't lawful, Shelter's free emergency helpline is 0808 800 4444 (England and Scotland, Monday to Friday). In Wales, Shelter Cymru's advice line is 08000 495 495; in Northern Ireland, Housing Rights is on 028 9024 5640. If you're in immediate danger, call 999.
