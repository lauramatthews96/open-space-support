Who Am I, If I'm Not That Anymore?

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

There's a particular kind of dizziness that comes with realising you're not entirely sure who you are underneath everything you were taught to be. Not in a dramatic, existential way, just a quiet, unsettling sense that a lot of what you thought was "you" might actually have been shaped, deliberately, by something outside you.

Your opinions on things. The way you dress, or used to. What you found funny, or weren't allowed to find funny. What you wanted from life, and what you were taught to want instead. If you grew up in a high-control environment, or spent a long time inside one, it can be genuinely hard to tell which of these were ever really yours.

That's not a sign of a weak or empty personality. It's what happens when a group works hard, over years, to shape a person's identity to fit its own purposes. The fact that it worked doesn't mean there's nothing underneath it. It means you haven't had the chance yet to find out what's there.

Why this feels so disorienting

Most people build their sense of self gradually, through trial and error, trying things, changing their minds, being allowed to be inconsistent while they work it out. If you grew up in a high-control group, a lot of that experimentation may have been actively discouraged or made to feel dangerous. Curiosity outside approved lines could mean disapproval, guilt, or worse. So instead of a slow, low-stakes process of figuring out who you are, you may have had a fixed identity handed to you early, with real consequences for stepping outside it.

Leaving removes that structure all at once. Suddenly nothing is telling you what to think, wear, want, or believe, and that freedom can feel less like relief and more like standing in an empty room with no idea where to start. That confusion is common, and it's not something you're doing wrong.

Some of what you're feeling might not be about identity at all

It's worth separating two things that often get tangled together here. One is genuine identity, the deeper values, instincts, and preferences that are actually yours. The other is habit, the years of practised behaviour that became automatic simply through repetition, whether or not you ever agreed with it.

Some of what feels like "who you are" might really just be what you're used to doing. That's useful to know, because habits can change with time and practice, in a way that feels far less threatening than "figuring out my whole identity." You don't have to solve the big question all at once. You can just notice, in small moments, whether something feels like you or like leftover routine.

Small ways to start finding out

There's no single method for this, and it happens slowly, in pieces, usually without a clear before-and-after moment. A few things that tend to help along the way.

Notice reactions before you edit them. When you feel a strong opinion, a flash of enjoyment, or discomfort about something, try to catch that first honest reaction before you talk yourself into or out of it. That instinctive layer, underneath the explaining, is often closer to the real you than the polished version you present afterward.

Try things with no pressure to keep them. New music, new food, a different way of spending a Saturday. You're not committing to a new identity, you're just gathering information about what you actually respond to, separate from what you were told to respond to.

Give yourself permission to be inconsistent. You might like something one week and not the next. You might hold a belief for a while and then quietly drop it. That's not instability, that's what normal identity development looks like when it's finally allowed to happen.

Pay attention to what feels like relief. Certain decisions, once made, bring a kind of quiet settling feeling, even if they're small. That feeling is worth trusting more than you might expect.

You're allowed to keep some of it

This isn't about rejecting everything you were before leaving. Some of what you value, how you treat people, what you find meaningful, how you think about family, might genuinely still be true for you, chosen freely rather than imposed. Figuring out who you are now doesn't mean throwing out everything from before. It means going through it deliberately, keeping what's actually yours, and letting go of what was only ever expected of you.

There's no deadline on this

People sometimes feel embarrassed that they're still working this out a year, two years, five years after leaving, as though there's a point by which they should have arrived at a finished, settled sense of self. There isn't. Most people, whatever their background, are quietly revising who they are throughout their whole lives. You're just doing a more concentrated version of something everyone does anyway, and doing it without the usual scaffolding most people get to lean on along the way.

Whoever you find underneath all of it, take your time getting there.
