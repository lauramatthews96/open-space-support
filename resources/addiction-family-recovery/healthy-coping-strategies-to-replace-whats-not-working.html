Healthy Coping Strategies to Replace What's Not Working

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

If you've recognised that a coping pattern, whether that's alcohol, gambling, overworking, or something else, isn't serving you anymore, the natural next question is what actually replaces it. This is often the hardest, least talked about part of recovery, because "just stop" ignores the very real function the behaviour was serving in the first place. You weren't reaching for it randomly. It was doing a job, managing overwhelming feelings, filling a gap, offering relief, and that job doesn't disappear just because you decide to stop the behaviour that was doing it.

This article isn't a replacement for proper treatment or professional support, particularly if the pattern has become significant. But it is a starting point for thinking about what genuinely helps, rather than generic advice that often misses what's actually going on underneath.

Why "just distract yourself" often falls short

A lot of common coping advice focuses on distraction, go for a walk, watch something, keep busy. Distraction has its place, but it doesn't address the underlying feeling that the original behaviour was managing. If the coping mechanism was numbing grief, or managing anxiety, or providing a sense of control, then a strategy that only distracts, without addressing that underlying need, tends to work only temporarily, if at all.

More lasting coping strategies tend to do one of two things: they either genuinely process the underlying feeling, rather than just avoiding it, or they meet the same real need the old behaviour was meeting, but in a way that doesn't cause harm.

Matching the replacement to the actual function

If the old coping mechanism was about numbing overwhelming feelings, strategies that help you process feelings in smaller, more manageable amounts tend to help more than pure distraction. This might include journaling specifically about what you're feeling rather than just writing generally, talking to a therapist or trusted person regularly rather than only in a crisis, or structured practices like grounding techniques that help you stay present with difficult feelings without being overwhelmed by them.

If it was about a sense of control, look for genuinely controllable things to focus that need toward. Physical activity, learning a new skill, organising or completing tasks, anything that gives you a real, legitimate sense of agency and progress, tends to meet this need more sustainably than a behaviour where the sense of control is actually an illusion.

If it was about filling structural emptiness, routine itself can help, not a rigid, controlling routine, but a gentle, consistent structure to your days and weeks. Regular exercise, consistent sleep and meal times, a weekly commitment to something (a class, a volunteering slot, a regular call with someone), can rebuild some of the scaffolding that was lost without recreating the control that made the original environment harmful.

If it was about connection and belonging, and this is an especially common one after leaving a high-control group, finding genuine community, whether through a support group specifically for former members, a hobby-based group, or gradually rebuilding closeness with a smaller number of people, addresses this need directly, rather than through a substance or behaviour that only offers the feeling of connection temporarily.

Practical strategies worth trying

The urge surfing technique. When a strong urge hits, rather than immediately acting on it or fighting it, try simply noticing it, naming it ("this is an urge, it's uncomfortable, and it will pass"), and giving it a set amount of time, ten or fifteen minutes, before deciding whether to act on it. Urges genuinely do rise and fall like a wave, and riding that wave out, rather than fighting it or giving in immediately, weakens its grip over time.

Building a specific plan for difficult moments, rather than relying on willpower alone in the moment. Knowing in advance what you'll do when a craving or difficult feeling hits, call a specific person, go for a walk, do a particular grounding exercise, removes the need to make a good decision from scratch while already in a vulnerable state.

Physical movement, genuinely underrated. Even a short walk changes your physiological state in ways that can meaningfully reduce the intensity of a craving or a difficult emotional spike, not as a cure, but as a real, evidence-backed tool worth having available.

Connecting with someone, before you need to, not just in a crisis. Regular, ongoing connection with people who understand what you're going through, whether that's a support group, a therapist, or a trusted friend, builds a resource you can draw on, rather than only reaching out when things are already at a breaking point.

This is a skill, and skills take practice

None of these strategies will feel as immediately effective as the old coping mechanism did, at least not at first. That's not a sign they're not working, it's the nature of the shift you're making, trading something that gave fast relief for something that offers a slower, more sustainable kind of steadiness. Be patient with yourself here. Most people don't get this right every time, and a setback doesn't undo the progress you've already made. It just means you're human, in the middle of learning something genuinely difficult.

If self-help isn't enough on its own

This is a sensitive topic, and if a coping pattern feels bigger than you're able to manage with self-help strategies alone, please reach out for proper support.
