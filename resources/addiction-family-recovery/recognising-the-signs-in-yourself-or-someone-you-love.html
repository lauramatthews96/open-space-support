Recognising the Signs, in Yourself or Someone You Love

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

Recognising a developing addiction, in yourself or in someone close to you, is genuinely hard, partly because the early signs are often easy to explain away, and partly because nobody wants to believe it's happening, either to themselves or to someone they love. This isn't a diagnostic checklist, only a professional can properly assess that, but it's a starting point for noticing patterns worth paying closer attention to, without panic and without shame.

Why this can be hard to see clearly

Addiction rarely announces itself early on. It tends to build gradually, disguised as something more understandable, stress relief, a reward after a hard day, a way to relax, a coping tool that genuinely helped at first. That's part of what makes it hard to catch early, the behaviour often starts out looking completely reasonable, and it can be a long time before the pattern becomes distinct enough to name clearly.

If you're worried about yourself, there's often a strong pull toward minimising it, "it's not that bad," "I can stop whenever I want," "everyone does this sometimes." If you're worried about someone else, there's often a reluctance to say anything, not wanting to accuse, not wanting to damage the relationship, hoping it resolves on its own.

Both of these instincts are understandable. Neither of them tends to help much.

Signs worth paying attention to, in yourself

The behaviour is happening more often, or in larger amounts, than it used to, even if each individual instance still feels justified in the moment.

You've tried to cut back or stop, and found it harder than expected. Not necessarily a dramatic failure, sometimes just a quiet realisation that you meant to stop and didn't, more than once.

You're using it to manage feelings, not just to enjoy something. There's a real difference between choosing to do something because you want to, and reaching for it because you need relief from something uncomfortable underneath.

It's affecting other areas of your life, work, relationships, finances, health, even in small, easy to dismiss ways at first.

You feel defensive or secretive about it. If you find yourself minimising it to others, or actively hiding the extent of it, that defensiveness is often a more reliable signal than the behaviour itself.

You've noticed increasing tolerance, needing more of the behaviour to get the same sense of relief you used to get from less.

None of these, on their own, necessarily mean there's a serious problem. But if several of them are true, it's worth taking that seriously rather than continuing to explain it away.

Signs worth paying attention to, in someone you love

Noticeable changes in mood or behaviour, particularly increased irritability, secrecy, or withdrawal from previously normal activities and relationships.

Explanations that don't quite add up, about time, money, or behaviour, especially if you notice a pattern of minor inconsistencies rather than one isolated incident.

Financial changes that don't have a clear explanation. Unexplained spending, borrowing, or sudden financial strain can be a sign worth gently exploring, though it's not proof on its own.

Physical signs, changes in sleep, appearance, or health, that seem to correlate with the behaviour you're concerned about.

Defensiveness or anger when the topic comes up, even gently or indirectly. A disproportionate reaction to a mild question is sometimes more telling than the answer itself.

Your own growing sense of worry that you can't quite shake. Trust this instinct more than you might expect to. People close to someone struggling often sense something is wrong well before they have concrete evidence, and that instinct is usually picking up on something real.

What to do if you recognise these signs in yourself

Start by being honest with yourself, privately, without needing to have a whole plan ready yet. Naming the pattern accurately, even just to yourself, is a genuinely meaningful first step. From there, reaching out to a GP, a specialist service like FRANK or a local addiction support charity, or a trusted person in your life, is worth doing sooner rather than waiting for things to get worse. Our guide on what recovery pathways actually look like covers the practical next steps in more detail.

What to do if you recognise these signs in someone else

Approach the conversation with concern rather than accusation, if you can. "I've noticed some changes and I'm worried about you" tends to land better than a confrontation built around specific evidence, which can trigger defensiveness rather than openness. Be prepared for the conversation to not go perfectly the first time, denial is a common initial response, and that doesn't mean the conversation wasn't worth having.

It's also worth looking after your own wellbeing through this. Supporting someone through addiction is genuinely hard, and organisations like Adfam and Al-Anon (in our external resources) exist specifically to support family members and loved ones, not just the person struggling directly.

Noticing early is genuinely worth something

The earlier a developing pattern is recognised, honestly and without excessive shame, the more manageable it generally is to address. This isn't about catching every early sign perfectly or never missing anything. It's about staying willing to look honestly, in yourself and in the people you love, rather than looking away because it feels easier in the moment. That willingness, more than anything else, tends to make the biggest difference to what happens next.

If you're worried right now

This is a sensitive topic, and if you're worried about yourself or someone else right now, please reach out for support rather than carrying it alone. FRANK offers judgement-free advice on 0300 123 6600 (UK, free, confidential, 24/7), and our external resources include further specialist support for both individuals and families.
