Opening a Bank Account for the First Time

4 min read · Last updated August 2026

If you've never opened a bank account before, the whole process can feel oddly intimidating for something so ordinary. Everyone else seems to have done this years ago without thinking twice about it. You're stood in a branch, or staring at an app, wondering if you're about to say the wrong thing and get turned away.

You won't. This is more straightforward than it looks, and banks deal with people opening their first account all the time - you're far from the only one.

What you'll actually need

Most UK banks ask for two things: proof of identity and proof of address.

Proof of identity is usually a passport or a driving licence. If you don't have either yet, some banks accept a birth certificate alongside another form of ID, or a "vouching" system where someone who already banks with them confirms who you are. If you're stuck here, a few basic banks (Monzo and Starling are two commonly used ones) have simpler ID requirements and let you verify using just your phone camera and a short video - worth trying if a traditional bank turns you away.

Proof of address is usually a recent bill, a tenancy agreement, or a letter from a GP or council confirming where you live. If you're staying with someone else and nothing's in your name yet, a signed letter from them confirming you live there, alongside their own proof of address, is often accepted. Ask the bank directly what they'll take - it varies more than you'd expect, and reception staff are usually happy to talk you through options rather than just rejecting you.

Picking a bank

There isn't a wrong answer here, but a few things are worth knowing:

High street banks (Barclays, Lloyds, NatWest, HSBC and similar) give you a physical branch to walk into if something goes wrong, which some people find reassuring when everything else feels new.

App-based banks (Monzo, Starling, Revolut) are usually the fastest to set up - sometimes within minutes - and tend to have the most forgiving ID requirements. Everything happens through your phone, which suits some people and unsettles others.

Neither is better. It's genuinely about what makes you feel steadier.

The bit nobody warns you about: fees

Basic current accounts in the UK are free to open and free to run, as long as you stay within your agreed limits. The fees people get caught out by are usually:

Unarranged overdraft charges - going below zero without having arranged an overdraft first. These add up fast and catch a lot of first-time account holders off guard.

International transfer fees - if you're sending or receiving money abroad, ask about the exchange rate and fee before you do it, not after.

"Packaged" accounts - some banks try to upsell accounts with monthly fees in exchange for perks like travel insurance. You almost certainly don't need this yet. A free basic account does everything you need when you're starting out.

If a member of staff offers you an account with a monthly fee, it's completely fine to say "I'd like the free basic account, please" and leave it there. Nobody will argue with you.

What to actually do, step by step

  1. Decide between a branch-based bank or an app-based one.
  2. Gather whatever ID and address proof you have - even if it feels incomplete, take it in and ask what else they'll accept.
  3. If going into a branch, you can usually just walk in - most don't require an appointment for opening a basic account, though it's worth a quick call ahead to check.
  4. If going app-based, download the app and follow the prompts - most of this can be done in one sitting.
  5. Once open, set up online/app access straight away so you can see your balance without waiting for statements in the post.

If you get turned down somewhere

It happens, and it's not a reflection on you. ID and address requirements trip up a lot of people in exactly your situation, not because anyone's suspicious of you, but because the system is built around assuming everyone already has a paper trail. If one bank says no, try one of the app-based ones next - their requirements are often more flexible precisely because they're built for people who don't have a long-established address or credit history yet.

And if you're genuinely stuck, Citizens Advice can help with this specific problem - it's common enough that they have a well-worn process for it.

Getting a bank account open is one small, boring, practical thing. But it's also one less thing standing between you and everything else you're trying to build. Worth ticking off early.
