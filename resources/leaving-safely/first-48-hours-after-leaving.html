What to Expect in the First 48 Hours After You Leave

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

If you've just left, or you're about to, this one's for the bit nobody really prepares you for: not the planning, not the decision itself, but the strange, disorienting stretch right after. The first two days tend to look different to how people imagine them, and it can help to know that in advance.

It might feel like nothing at all, at first

A lot of people expect a wave of relief, or grief, or fear - something dramatic. What actually happens is often quieter and stranger than that. A kind of flatness. A sense of unreality, like you're watching yourself from slightly outside your own body. You might feel oddly calm, almost businesslike, sorting out practical things without much emotion attached.

That's not you being cold or in denial. It's a fairly normal response to a huge amount of change happening at once - your nervous system doing what it can to keep you functional while the rest of you catches up later. If the big emotional reaction doesn't arrive on schedule, that doesn't mean it isn't coming, and it doesn't mean something's wrong with you either way.

Practical things worth doing in the first day, if you can

You don't need to do all of this immediately, and some of it may already be sorted if you planned ahead. But if you're starting from very little, here's a rough order of priority.

Somewhere safe to be tonight. This comes before almost everything else. If you don't already have somewhere lined up, Shelter's helpline (0808 800 4444, England) can help with emergency housing options, and StreetLink can connect you with local outreach support if you're at risk of sleeping rough. Neither of these require you to explain your situation in detail - just that you need somewhere safe.

Let one person know where you are, if there's anyone you trust enough to tell. Not for permission, just so someone outside your own head knows your situation. If you don't have that person yet, a helpline serves the same purpose.

Basic essentials, if you left without them - phone charger, a change of clothes, any medication you take regularly. If money's tight right now, that's common and nothing to feel ashamed of; local council welfare assistance schemes and food banks exist for exactly this kind of situation.

Beyond that, genuinely, it's okay to just get through the day. Everything else can wait.

The emotional weather will probably be unpredictable

Over the first 48 hours, it's common to swing between feelings that don't seem to fit together - relief and terror within the same hour, grief and something close to euphoria, exhaustion and a strange restless energy. None of that means you're doing this wrong. It usually just means you're feeling the actual size of what's happened, in pieces, because feeling it all at once would be too much.

Try not to judge whatever comes up. If you feel numb, that's fine. If you feel like celebrating, that's fine too, even if it seems like an odd reaction given everything. There's no correct emotional response to leaving, and there's no timeline you're supposed to be following.

You might hear from people trying to reach you

Depending on your situation, you may get calls, messages, or visits from family or former community members in the first day or two - sometimes concerned, sometimes pressuring you to return or explain yourself. You don't owe anyone an explanation right now, and you're allowed to not respond at all, or to respond only when you're ready.

If contact starts to feel overwhelming or unsafe, it's completely reasonable to mute notifications, block numbers temporarily, or ask a trusted person to screen messages for you for the first little while. This isn't cutting anyone off forever - it's giving yourself room to breathe before you decide how, or whether, to engage.

Talking to someone who understands

This is a good moment to reach out to people who've actually been through this specific experience, rather than trying to explain it from scratch to someone who hasn't. Faith to Faithless, run by Humanists UK, is a UK helpline specifically for people who've left high-control religious groups, including a strong track record of supporting people who've left the Jehovah's Witnesses - the volunteers understand exactly what you're describing without needing background explained. Their peer support groups can also be a good next step once the immediate dust has settled.

Our own Support Application Portal is another route in, if you'd rather start there - no pressure to have anything figured out before you reach out.

You don't have to have a five-year plan by Wednesday

The pressure to immediately have a job, a flat, a routine, a whole new life sorted can feel intense, especially if part of you is anxious to prove the decision was right. It wasn't a mistake to leave without having everything worked out. Almost nobody does. The first 48 hours are about getting through the first 48 hours - safely, with somewhere to sleep and at least one person who knows where you are. Everything else has time to come together.

If you need help right now

If you're in immediate danger, please contact emergency services on 999. Faith to Faithless (Humanists UK) offers free, confidential support specifically for people leaving high-control religious groups.
