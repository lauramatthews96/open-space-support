Networking Without a Network

5 min read · Last updated August 2026

A lot of career advice assumes you've already got some kind of network to work with, family friends in useful industries, old school contacts, a wider circle you can casually mention "I'm looking for work" to. If your entire social world was inside a high-control group, that assumption doesn't hold, and advice built on it can feel useless or even a little alienating. You're not doing this wrong. You're starting from a genuinely different position, and it's worth building an approach that actually fits it.

What networking actually is, underneath the word

Networking sounds transactional, like working a room trying to extract favours from strangers. In practice, for most people, it's much smaller and more human than that. It's mostly just talking to people, staying in touch with people you've met, and being someone others think of when an opportunity comes up. You don't need a polished elevator pitch or a stack of business cards. You need a handful of genuine, low-pressure connections, built gradually.

Starting points that don't require an existing network

People you already know now, however you know them. Colleagues, however briefly you've worked with them. Neighbours. People from a course or class you've taken. People from any community group, hobby, or activity you're part of, even loosely. This is often a bigger starting point than it feels like, because you likely already know more people than you're giving yourself credit for.

Volunteering. Genuinely one of the best routes into meeting people connected to work you might be interested in, since it puts you alongside people already active in a field, in a low-pressure setting where nobody expects anything transactional from you.

Local community and skills groups. Job clubs, adult education classes, library-run career sessions, and charity-run employment programmes (see our external resources for some starting points) put you in a room with other people navigating similar territory, and often with volunteers or mentors from relevant industries too.

Online communities. LinkedIn is the obvious one for professional networking specifically, but industry-specific forums, local Facebook groups, and even hobby communities related to a field you're interested in can lead somewhere useful, often more naturally than a formal networking event would.

How to actually talk to people, without it feeling forced

Ask questions, don't pitch yourself. People generally respond better to genuine curiosity about their work than to a rehearsed introduction about you. "How did you get into this?" or "What's a typical day like for you?" tends to open a real conversation, and it takes the pressure off you to perform.

Follow up, briefly. If you have a good conversation with someone, a short message afterward, "really enjoyed hearing about your work, thanks for taking the time," keeps the connection alive without asking for anything. This alone puts you ahead of a lot of people who never follow up at all.

It's fine to eventually ask directly. Once you've built even a small amount of rapport, asking "do you know of anywhere hiring for X" or "would you be willing to have a quick call about how you got started" is a completely normal, expected part of how this works. People are often more willing to help than you'd expect, especially if you've been genuine rather than transactional up to that point.

Quality matters more than quantity. You don't need fifty contacts. A handful of people who genuinely know you and think well of you is worth more than a long list of names who barely remember meeting you.

LinkedIn, briefly

If you're new to LinkedIn, it can feel like an odd, performative space at first. A simple approach: a clear, friendly profile photo, a short summary of what you're looking for or working toward, and then genuinely engaging, commenting thoughtfully on posts in a field you're interested in, connecting with people you've actually spoken to rather than mass-adding strangers. It builds slowly, the same way any other networking does, just in a different setting.

You're not behind, you're building this from scratch, which is different

It can feel discouraging watching other people rely on networks that took no visible effort to build, family connections, old friendships, a wide circle from years of overlapping social settings. But a network built deliberately, one genuine connection at a time, tends to be more durable than one that was simply inherited, because every part of it exists because you actually built it. It takes longer to get started.

It doesn't take longer to become genuinely useful, once it exists.
