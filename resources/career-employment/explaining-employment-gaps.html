Explaining Employment Gaps in Interviews

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

There's a particular kind of dread that comes with the question "so, tell me about this gap on your CV." You know it's coming. You've probably rehearsed an answer in the shower, in the car, lying awake at 2am the night before the interview. And then you get in the room and it still comes out wrong.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: employers ask about gaps because they're curious, not because they're trying to catch you out. Most of the time, anyway. They want to know you've got a reasonable explanation and that you're not going to vanish on them again. That's it. That's the whole bar.

You don't need a perfect story. You need a true one, told with some confidence.

What they're actually listening for

When someone asks about a gap, they're not asking for your life story. They're asking three quiet questions underneath the obvious one:

Did something happen that would make you unreliable? Are you going to be honest with me about it, or dodge the question? And are you actually ready to be here now?

If you can answer those three things - even briefly - you've done your job. The specifics of what happened during the gap matter far less than how steady you sound talking about it.

You don't owe anyone your whole story

This is the bit that trips people up most. There's a difference between being honest and being an open book, and you're allowed to choose the second one without it being a lie.

"I left a religious community I'd been part of since childhood, and it took me some time to get my life set up - housing, ID, that sort of thing" is true. It's honest. It also tells an interviewer exactly what they need to know without inviting them into twenty years of your history. You don't have to mention Jehovah's Witnesses by name if you don't want to. You don't have to explain shunning, or disfellowshipping, or why you don't speak to half your family. None of that is required information for a job interview, and a decent interviewer won't push for it.

If they do push - and occasionally someone will, out of nosiness rather than malice - a simple "it's a personal story, but I'm in a really settled place now" tends to close that door without any awkwardness on your end.

A way to frame it that actually works

Here's a rough shape that a lot of people find useful. It's not a script - please don't memorise it word for word, you'll sound like you're reciting something - but it's a structure to build your own version around.

  1. Name it briefly. One sentence. "I went through a significant personal transition that meant stepping away from work for a while."
  2. Say what you did with the time, if anything. This bit matters more than people think. Did you retrain? Volunteer? Look after a relative? Even "I spent time getting stable - housing, finances, that kind of thing" is a real answer. You don't need a bootcamp certificate to justify a gap.
  3. Bring it back to now. This is the part that actually gets you the job. "I'm in a settled place, I'm genuinely excited about this role, and I'm ready to commit to it." Say it like you mean it, because presumably you do.

That's three sentences. You can say that in twenty seconds and move on with your dignity intact.

What if they ask follow-up questions?

Some will. Most won't push past a polite "thanks for sharing that, must have been tough" and move on to the next question, because most interviewers are more focused on the job than your backstory. But if you get someone who keeps digging, it's fine to gently redirect.

"I'd rather not go into the details, but I'm happy to talk about what I bring to this role" is a completely acceptable sentence to say out loud in an interview. You're not being difficult. You're setting a boundary, which - incidentally - is a perfectly good skill to demonstrate to an employer anyway.

The gap is not the whole interview

It's easy to let this one question loom over everything else, but realistically it's one question in a forty-minute conversation. Prepare your answer, practise saying it out loud until it doesn't feel like a confession, and then let it go. Put your energy into the parts of the interview where you actually get to talk about what you're good at.

Because here's the truth most people leaving a high-control environment don't get told often enough: you've already done something genuinely difficult. Walking away from an entire structured life and rebuilding it from close to nothing takes a kind of resilience that a lot of candidates simply haven't had to develop. You don't need to say that in the interview. But it's worth remembering it on the drive there.

A few practical lines, if you want them

If none of the above feels natural yet, here are a few ready-made options. Take whichever fits, change the wording until it sounds like you, and use it.

  • "I was part of a community I later chose to leave, which took some time to fully step away from. I'm well past that now."
  • "There was a period of significant personal change - I was rebuilding a lot of things at once. I came out the other side of it in a good position."
  • "I took time away for personal reasons that I'd rather keep private, but I'm in a stable, ready place to start something new."

None of these are lies. All of them are true without being an overshare. Pick the one that sits easiest in your mouth, say it a few times until it stops feeling stiff, and then go in and get the job.
