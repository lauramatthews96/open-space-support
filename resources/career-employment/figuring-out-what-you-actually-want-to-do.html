Figuring Out What You Actually Want to Do

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

Most people get years of low-stakes practice figuring out what they enjoy and what they're good at, school subjects they gravitated toward, part-time jobs that gave them a taste of different environments, hobbies that hinted at a direction. If you didn't get much of that, or if your options growing up were narrowed deliberately, you might be starting your career with a genuinely open question in front of you: what do I actually want to do, rather than what am I allowed to do, or what's simply available right now.

That's a real gap, and it's worth taking seriously rather than just grabbing the first job that appears and hoping it works out. You've got more time and more choice than the pressure of the moment might suggest.

Why this question can feel harder than it should

If your environment discouraged ambition beyond a narrow, approved set of paths, or treated certain kinds of work as more "acceptable" than others, you may never have had the chance to notice your own preferences building up over time. On top of that, financial pressure after leaving can make the question feel like a luxury you don't have time for, get any job, sort out the "what do I actually want" question later.

Both of those things are real. But even a rough sense of direction, built gradually alongside taking whatever work is currently available, makes a genuine difference to where you end up in a few years, rather than drifting from one unrelated job to the next without ever building toward something.

Start by noticing, not deciding

You don't need to answer "what's my career" in one sitting. It's more useful to collect small pieces of information about yourself over time, the way you'd gradually get to know a new city rather than trying to memorise a map of it in one go.

Notice what you enjoy doing, even outside of work context. Organising things. Talking to people. Solving practical problems. Working with your hands. Explaining things to others. These instincts, wherever they show up, often point toward genuine strengths, even if you've never framed them as career-relevant before.

Notice what drains you versus what energises you, in whatever work or responsibilities you've already had, paid or otherwise. This tells you more about direction than any personality quiz.

Pay attention to envy, carefully. If you hear about someone's job and feel a small pull of "that sounds interesting," that's worth taking seriously as data, rather than dismissing it as unrealistic before you've even looked into it.

Try things at low stakes before committing

You don't need to pick a lifelong career path on your first attempt. Trying a job, a course, or a volunteering role and discovering it's not for you isn't a failure, it's genuinely useful information, and it's a completely normal part of how most people find their direction, whatever their background.

Short courses and taster sessions exist for a huge range of fields, often free or low-cost through adult education centres or online platforms, and they're a low-pressure way to get a feel for something before committing further.

Volunteering can offer a similar taste of a field, whether that's healthcare, admin, working with animals, events, or something else entirely, without the pressure of it being your actual livelihood yet.

Informational conversations, simply asking someone already working in a field what their day-to-day actually looks like, are more accessible than people expect. Most people are happy to talk about their own job if you ask politely and briefly.

It's fine to not know yet, and to change your mind later

There's no deadline on figuring this out completely, and plenty of people, regardless of background, change direction more than once over their working life. If right now the honest answer is "I don't know yet, I just need an income," that's a completely legitimate starting point. Taking a job for practical reasons now doesn't lock you into it forever, and it can still teach you something useful about what you do and don't want next.

A rough way to think about it

If it helps to have some structure, try roughly separating three questions rather than trying to answer one big one at once: What am I reasonably good at, based on real evidence so far. What do I actually enjoy, based on genuine reaction rather than what sounds impressive. And what would realistically support the life I want, financially and otherwise, right now.

You won't get a perfect overlap of all three straight away. Most people don't. But even a rough sense of where those three things might meet gives you something to move toward, rather than just reacting to whatever comes along next.

This is allowed to take a while

Building a genuine sense of direction, especially if you're starting later than most or without the practice others had, is a real process, not a single decision. Be patient with yourself here.

The fact that you're asking the question at all, rather than just accepting the first thing available without a second thought, is already a meaningful step most people never consciously take.
