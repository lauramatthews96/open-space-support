Discovering What You Actually Enjoy

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

If a lot of what you liked before was shaped, encouraged, or required by an environment you've since left, it's genuinely reasonable to reach a point where you're not sure what you actually enjoy, separate from what you were told to enjoy, or simply had access to. This isn't a small or trivial question. Taste, the things that bring you genuine pleasure, curiosity, or absorption, is a real part of who you are, and it's worth rebuilding deliberately rather than just assuming you'll stumble into it eventually.

Why this can feel harder than it sounds

Most people build a sense of their own taste gradually, through years of exposure to lots of different things, some encouraged, some discovered by accident, with room to try things and simply decide they don't like them, without much consequence. If your exposure to music, media, hobbies, or ways of spending free time was limited or filtered, you may be starting this process later, and more deliberately, than most people ever have to.

On top of that, if enjoyment for its own sake was ever treated as frivolous, unnecessary, or something to feel guilty about, you might notice a flicker of resistance even now, when you try to sit down and simply enjoy something with no purpose beyond the enjoyment itself. That resistance is worth noticing, and gently setting aside, rather than obeying automatically.

Start by casting a wide, low-pressure net

You don't need to find your grand passion immediately. The goal, at first, is simply exposure, trying things without much investment, so you can notice your actual reactions rather than guessing at them in the abstract.

Try things you've simply never had access to before. Different genres of music, films, books, food, activities, without worrying yet about whether you'll like them. The trying is the point, not the outcome.

Say yes to low-stakes invitations, even ones that don't obviously appeal to you on paper. A friend's hobby, a local class, an activity you'd normally dismiss without thinking about it. Some of the most useful discoveries come from things you wouldn't have chosen deliberately.

Revisit things you weren't allowed to properly explore before, if any of those come to mind. Sometimes there's a flicker of old curiosity about something specific, worth taking seriously now that it's actually available to you.

Notice, don't judge. If something doesn't land, that's useful information too, not a failure. You're gathering data about your actual preferences, and "I tried that and didn't enjoy it" is just as valuable a discovery as finding something you love.

Pay attention to the physical signals, not just the thoughts

Genuine enjoyment often shows up in the body before it shows up as a clear thought. A slight lean forward, a loss of track of time, a feeling of lightness or absorption. These are often more reliable indicators of real interest than your immediate conscious opinion, which might still be filtered through old ideas about what you're supposed to like or find worthwhile.

If you notice yourself getting absorbed in something, even something small or seemingly unimportant, that's worth paying attention to and following further, rather than dismissing it because it doesn't fit a bigger, more meaningful-sounding category of interest.

It's fine, and normal, to change your mind

You might enjoy something intensely for a while and then lose interest completely. That's not a sign of an unstable personality, it's what normal interest and taste actually look like for most people, evolving and shifting over time rather than fixed forever from the first discovery. Give yourself permission to move on from things without needing a justification.

Guilt might show up, and it's worth naming when it does

If you notice guilt creeping in around something purely enjoyable, spending time or money on a hobby, choosing rest over productivity, enjoying something with no practical benefit, try to name it directly. "This is old conditioning telling me pleasure needs to be justified. It doesn't." You don't have to fully believe that the first time you say it. Repeating it, and acting in spite of the guilt anyway, is how the belief actually shifts over time.

This adds up to something real, even slowly

Rebuilding a genuine sense of your own taste and enjoyment isn't a single discovery, it's an accumulation of small moments of noticing what actually lights you up, over months and years, not one dramatic realisation. Every small thing you try, like or dislike, keep or discard, adds a little more definition to a picture of yourself that's genuinely yours, built by you, rather than handed to you. That picture is worth taking your time over. There's no deadline on finding out who you are when you're finally free to choose.
