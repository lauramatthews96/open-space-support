Celebrating How Far You've Come

5 min read · Last updated August 2026

If you've spent the last while rebuilding your life, work, money, relationships, a sense of who you are, it's easy to move from one challenge straight into the next without ever really pausing to notice how much has actually changed. Rebuilding tends to feel like a long list of things still left to do, rather than a story of real, significant progress already made. This article is a deliberate pause, an invitation to look back honestly at how far you've actually come, because that recognition matters more than it might seem to right now.

Why this is easy to skip, and worth doing anyway

When you're in the middle of rebuilding, the natural focus is forward, what's still unresolved, what still needs sorting, what still feels hard. That forward focus is useful for getting things done, but it also means progress can pass by almost unnoticed, because as soon as one thing improves, attention moves straight to the next unresolved thing, without ever properly registering what was just accomplished.

This isn't unique to your situation, most people underrate their own progress, generally. But it's often more pronounced after leaving a high-control environment, because so much has had to be rebuilt at once, and because there's rarely anyone in your life with the full picture to reflect your progress back to you the way family might for someone with a more conventional life story.

A way to actually do this, not just an idea to agree with

Reading that reflection matters is one thing. Actually doing it is another. Here's a genuinely practical way to try it.

Think back to a specific point after leaving, a month in, six months in, a year in, whatever feels like a meaningful marker. Not the general idea of "back then," but a specific moment you can picture clearly.

Write down, honestly, what your life actually looked like then. What you knew, what you didn't. What felt possible, what felt completely out of reach. Be specific rather than vague, specific details make the comparison land more clearly than a general sense of "it was hard."

Now write down where you actually are now, in the same specific terms. Not where you wish you were, where you genuinely are, including things that might feel small or unremarkable to you now, precisely because they've become normal.

Read both side by side. This is usually the part that actually shifts something. Seeing the two versions next to each other, concretely, tends to reveal progress that's easy to miss when you're only ever looking forward.

Progress that's easy to overlook

A lot of real, significant progress doesn't feel like an achievement in the moment, because it's become your new baseline rather than something you're actively celebrating. Things worth actually counting, even if they now feel completely unremarkable to you:

Understanding how to manage your own money, even imperfectly. Having a CV, a bank account, a routine that's entirely your own. Making a decision, any decision, without needing permission first. Trying something new and discovering whether you liked it, on your own terms. A single honest conversation you had that would have felt impossible before. A day that passed without the old fear you used to carry constantly in the background.

None of these need to be dramatic to count. The fact that they're now simply part of your life, rather than a distant hope, is the actual measure of how far you've come.

You're allowed to feel proud, even if it feels awkward

If pride, in yourself specifically, feels uncomfortable or unfamiliar, that discomfort is worth noticing rather than obeying. A lot of people in your position were taught, directly or indirectly, that focusing on personal achievement was self-centred or spiritually risky. Letting yourself feel genuine pride in what you've built, even briefly, even if it feels a little strange at first, is a real and important part of claiming this life as fully your own.

This doesn't mean the hard parts are over

Acknowledging real progress doesn't require pretending everything is resolved, or that there's nothing difficult left ahead. Both things are true at once, there is still work to do, and you have already done something genuinely significant to get here. Holding both of these, rather than only focusing on what's unfinished, gives you a more accurate, and considerably kinder, picture of where you actually stand.

Come back to this, more than once

This isn't a one-time exercise. Revisiting it every few months, or at meaningful points along the way, gives you an ongoing, honest record of your own progress, one that's easy to lose sight of otherwise, buried under the everyday business of continuing to rebuild. You've already come further than you probably give yourself credit for. It's worth actually stopping, every so often, to see that clearly.
