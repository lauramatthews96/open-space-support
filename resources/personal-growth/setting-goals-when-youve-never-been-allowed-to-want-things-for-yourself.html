Setting Goals When You've Never Been Allowed to Want Things for Yourself

6 min read · Last updated August 2026

If your sense of purpose and direction was previously defined for you, by a group's expectations, a set of approved life paths, or simply a lack of encouragement to want anything beyond what was already mapped out, setting your own goals now can feel strangely difficult, even paralysing. Not because you lack ambition, but because "what do I actually want for my own life" may be a genuinely new question, one you've never had real practice answering.

Why this can feel disorienting rather than exciting

Goal-setting advice usually assumes you already have some rough sense of direction, and just need help organising it into steps. If you're starting from a much earlier point, not knowing what you want at all, that advice can feel like it's skipping the actual hard part. This isn't a failure of ambition or drive. It's simply a different, earlier starting point than most goal-setting frameworks are built for.

There's also often an emotional layer underneath the practical one. If wanting things for yourself, rather than for the group, the family, or some larger approved purpose, was ever treated as selfish or spiritually risky, you might feel a genuine flicker of guilt or anxiety even now, just from asking yourself what you want.

Start smaller than "goals," with wants

Before jumping to formal goals, it can help to spend time simply noticing wants, in their smallest, most immediate form, without judging whether they're big enough, important enough, or sensible enough to count.

I want to try that class. I want a quieter flat. I want to learn to cook properly. I want more time outdoors. None of these need to be dressed up as a five-year plan yet. They're just honest data points about what actually appeals to you, and noticing them without immediately filtering them is real practice for a skill you may not have built much of before.

A gentle way to build toward bigger goals

Notice patterns in your smaller wants. After a while of simply noticing what appeals to you, you'll often start to see themes, a pull toward creativity, toward stability, toward connection, toward independence, toward learning. These patterns are more trustworthy starting points for bigger goals than trying to invent a big goal from nothing.

Ask what would feel meaningful, not just impressive. It's easy to default to goals that sound good to other people, a certain job title, a certain achievement, rather than ones that would actually feel meaningful to you personally. Try to notice the difference when it comes up, and lean toward the ones that genuinely matter to you, even if they sound smaller or less impressive from the outside.

Let goals be provisional, not permanent commitments. A goal doesn't have to be a lifelong decision. Treating it as "this is what I want to work toward for now, and I can change my mind later" removes a lot of the pressure that can make goal-setting feel paralysing when you're not used to choosing for yourself at all.

Break it down into something you can start this week. Big goals can feel abstract and overwhelming, especially if wanting things at all still feels unfamiliar. A concrete, small first step, one class, one conversation, one hour set aside, makes the goal feel real and achievable rather than theoretical.

Watch for goals that are actually still someone else's

It's worth checking in occasionally about whose voice a particular goal is really coming from. Sometimes a goal that feels like your own is actually still shaped by old expectations, proving something, avoiding disapproval, meeting a standard that was never really yours to begin with. This doesn't mean the goal is automatically wrong, but it's worth noticing the source honestly, since goals built on obligation tend to feel very different, heavier, less sustaining, than ones built on genuine desire.

Guilt or anxiety around wanting things is common, and it eases with practice

If pursuing something purely because you want it, rather than because it serves some larger purpose or earns approval, brings up discomfort, that's a genuinely common response, not a sign you're doing something wrong. Like most learned patterns, it tends to soften with repetition rather than with a single moment of realisation. Each time you choose something for yourself, notice the discomfort, and do it anyway, you're building genuine evidence that wanting things for your own sake is allowed.

You're allowed to want things simply because you want them

This might be the single most important thing to sit with here. You don't need your goals to serve a bigger cause, prove something, or meet anyone else's definition of a worthwhile life. Wanting something purely because it would make your life better, richer, or more enjoyable is a completely legitimate reason on its own. Learning to trust that, and to act on it, is itself one of the most meaningful parts of building a life that's genuinely yours.
